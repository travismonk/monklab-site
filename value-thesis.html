Why Byblos

Value Thesis

The modern browser is remarkably good at what it was designed to do. It loads webpages quickly, gets out of your way, and makes it easy to move from one destination to the next. For many activities, that is exactly what you want.

Byblos exists because some activities do not end when the page loads. Sometimes the difficult part is not finding information. The difficult part is remembering why it mattered, comparing it to other information, and discussing it with other people. Those are the problems that Byblos solves.

Context Is Easy To Lose

Imagine planning a vacation. You spend several evenings looking through Airbnb listings. You find places you like, places you dislike, and places that seem promising but require more research. A week later you return to the search.

The listings are still there. What is harder to recover is everything surrounding them. Which listing was the one with the great view? Which one was your spouse excited about? Which one was surprisingly affordable? Which one looked perfect until you discovered the noisy road nearby? Which one did you ultimately reject, and why?

The same thing happens during research. You collect articles, videos, papers, and sources over days or weeks. Months later, you can usually find the material again. What is harder to recover is the reasoning process. Which sources did you trust? Which ones did you dismiss? What questions were you trying to answer at the time? What conclusions did you reach?

The page survived. The context often did not.

Byblos is built around the idea that the context surrounding a page can be just as important as the page itself. The tree preserves how you arrived somewhere. Annotations preserve what you thought about it. Discussions preserve what other people thought about it. Instead of separating pages from context, Byblos keeps them together.

Browsers Are Not Decision-Making Tools

Many important decisions involve webpages. Buying a car, planning a trip, choosing a school, comparing vendors, researching a medical condition, evaluating job candidates, and investigating a technical problem all involve gathering information from many different pages and comparing it over time.

The browser's answer is usually the same: open more tabs.

Eventually you end up with dozens of pages and a growing sense that you are losing track of the comparison. Most people compensate by introducing other tools. Notes move into one application. Discussions move into another. Costs and measurements end up in a spreadsheet. Reminders end up somewhere else entirely.

None of those tools are bad. In fact, each of them is useful. The problem is that the decision-making process becomes fragmented across multiple systems. The pages live in one place, your notes live somewhere else, and the conversation that led to the final decision lives somewhere else again.

Byblos takes a different approach. Ratings, notes, discussions, keywords, measurements, reminders, and reactions stay attached to the pages they describe. Sort and filter can then use that information to help organize large collections of pages. Instead of treating webpages as isolated destinations, Byblos treats them as part of a larger decision-making process.

Browsing Is Sometimes Social

Most browsing should remain private. Your finances should be private. Your medical records should be private. Your personal searches should be private. Browsers are very good at protecting those experiences, and Byblos is not trying to change that.

At the same time, a surprising amount of browsing is naturally collaborative. Families plan vacations together. Friends compare apartments together. Researchers collect sources together. Teams investigate problems together. Colleagues evaluate products and vendors together. In all of these situations, the web contains the information, but the collaboration usually happens somewhere else.

Links are copied into chat applications. Discussions become detached from the pages being discussed. Notes become separated from the source material. Weeks later, people struggle to remember the conclusion or reconstruct how they arrived at it.

Byblos does not force browsing to become social. It simply provides an affordance for social browsing when social browsing is useful. Pages can be shared. Discussions can happen on the pages themselves. Collaborators can follow the same tree, see the same context, and understand how a conclusion was reached. Privacy remains available when you want it. Collaboration becomes available when you need it.

The Big Idea

The internet already does a fantastic job of helping people find information. Byblos helps people preserve context, make decisions, and collaborate around that information over time.

A webpage is often only the beginning of the story. The interesting part is what people thought about it, how it compared to alternatives, and what decisions eventually emerged from it. Byblos exists to help preserve that part of the story as well.
